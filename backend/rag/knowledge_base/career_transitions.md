# Career Transitions and Job Search Strategy

## Changing field without starting over

Candidates moving into technology from another field consistently undervalue what they already have. A transition is not a reset; it is a repositioning.

The three assets that transfer:

**Domain knowledge.** A pharmacist moving into health technology, or an accountant moving into fintech, understands the users and the regulatory constraints in a way that a computer science graduate does not. Employers in that vertical pay for this. Target the industry you came from rather than treating your history as irrelevant.

**Professional maturity.** Someone with five years in any structured workplace knows how to handle a deadline, escalate a problem, and communicate with a stakeholder. Junior technical hires frequently do not. Say this explicitly in the summary line rather than hoping it is inferred.

**Adjacent technical skill.** Financial modelling is data work. Managing a supply chain is systems thinking. Name the overlap in the language of the target role.

The CV for a transition leads with a summary that states the target explicitly, followed by a projects section, then relevant technical skills, and only then prior employment reframed for its transferable content.

## Realistic timelines

A full transition into a first technical role typically takes nine to eighteen months of consistent part-time work for someone starting without a technical background. Candidates who expect three months usually quit at month four.

Shorter transitions are possible in adjacent moves: QA to development, support to systems administration, analyst to data analyst. These take three to six months because the domain context is already there.

## Job search as a process

Treat the search as a pipeline with measurable stages, because that is the only way to identify where it is failing.

Track four numbers: applications sent, screens obtained, technical rounds reached, offers received.

Diagnose from the ratios:

- **Many applications, few screens.** The problem is the CV or the targeting. You are applying to roles you are not credible for, or the CV fails to make the case. Fix the CV first; it is cheaper than applying more.
- **Screens but no technical rounds.** The problem is how you talk about your experience, or a mismatch on salary or logistics surfacing early.
- **Technical rounds but no offers.** The problem is technical depth or interview performance. This is the most fixable stage with focused practice.
- **Final rounds but no offers.** Usually communication or perceived fit rather than technical skill. Ask for feedback.

Sending more applications when the CV is the bottleneck is the most common wasted effort in a job search.

## Volume and targeting

A tailored application to a well-matched role converts far better than ten generic ones. But a search that only targets perfect matches stalls, because perfect matches are rare.

A workable split: roughly a quarter of applications to roles where you exceed the requirements, half to roles where you meet most of them, and a quarter to roles that are a stretch. The stretch applications occasionally succeed, and they reveal what the next level requires.

Apply to postings where you meet around sixty percent of the listed requirements. Requirement lists are written as wish lists, and employers hire candidates who meet most of them regularly.

## Handling gaps and rejections

An employment gap is not disqualifying; an unexplained one is. State the reason in one line and move on. Do not apologise for it or over-explain.

Rejection at scale is normal in a job search and carries almost no information about a single candidate. Response rates in the range of five to ten percent for cold applications are typical, which means twenty applications producing one or two conversations is functioning as expected, not failing.

The exception worth acting on: a pattern of rejection at the same stage across many processes indicates a specific fixable problem. Diagnose by stage rather than treating each rejection as a separate event.

## Networking without a network

Most candidates hear "networking" and imagine asking strangers for jobs. The version that works is narrower and easier.

Ask for information, not for a job. A short, specific message to an engineer at a target company asking what their team's stack looks like, or what they wish junior applicants understood, gets answered surprisingly often. A message asking for a referral from someone who has never spoken to you does not.

Contribute publicly. Answering questions in a technical community, writing up something you built, or contributing a small fix to an open-source project makes you findable and gives a referrer something to point at.

Attend events in person where they exist. Conversion from a face-to-face conversation to a referral is dramatically higher than from an online message.

## When to stay rather than move

Not every dissatisfaction is solved by changing employer. Staying is the better move when you are still learning quickly, when a specific promotion or project is within a defined and credible timeline, or when you have been in the role under a year and the CV would show a pattern of short tenures.

Leaving is the better move when learning has plateaued for six months or more, when compensation is materially below market and an internal correction has been refused, or when the work no longer builds toward where you want to be. Two short tenures in a row are explainable; four are a pattern that employers screen on.
