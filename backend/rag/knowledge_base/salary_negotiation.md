# Salary Negotiation

## Never name the first number

The single most costly mistake is stating a salary expectation before knowing the employer's band. Whoever names a figure first sets the anchor, and candidates consistently anchor below what the employer had budgeted.

When asked early for your expectation, redirect once:

"I'd rather understand the scope of the role first. Could you share the band you have budgeted for this position?"

Most recruiters will answer, because they are also screening for budget fit and want to avoid wasting the process. If pressed a second time, give a researched range rather than a single number, with the bottom of your range set at the figure you would actually accept happily.

## Research before the conversation

Enter the conversation with three data points:

1. What the role pays in your market at your experience level, from recent listings and salary reports rather than from anything older than a year.
2. What you currently earn, including bonuses and benefits, expressed as a total figure.
3. Your walk-away number, decided in advance and in writing, so it is not renegotiated under pressure in the moment.

Verify market figures against current sources. Salary data ages faster than most candidates assume, and quoting a stale figure undermines your credibility.

## Negotiating the offer

An initial offer is rarely the maximum. Employers generally hold some margin, and a professional counter does not withdraw an offer. The risk candidates fear here is largely imagined.

A counter has three parts: appreciation, a specific number, and a justification tied to value rather than need.

"Thank you, I'm glad to receive this and I want to accept. Based on what I found for this level in the market and the fact that I'd be taking on the payments integration work we discussed, I was targeting X. Is there room to get closer to that?"

Justify with market data and scope. Never justify with personal expenses; the employer is buying a capability, not funding a budget.

## What else is negotiable

When base salary is genuinely fixed, other terms often are not:

- Signing bonus, which frequently comes from a different budget line than salary.
- Job title, which affects your next role's starting point more than most candidates realise.
- Review timing, such as a formal salary review at six months instead of twelve.
- Remote or hybrid days.
- Training budget, conference attendance, and certification funding.
- Notice period and start date.
- Festival bonuses and their number, where these are customary.

Ask which of these has flexibility rather than requesting all of them.

## Getting it in writing

Verbal promises about future raises, promotions, or review timing are unenforceable and are frequently forgotten when the manager who made them leaves. Anything material should appear in the offer letter. Requesting this politely is normal and is not read as distrust.

Do not resign from a current position until a signed written offer is in hand.

## Raises in your current role

The internal raise conversation is different from an offer negotiation because you have less leverage and more information.

Build the case over months, not in the meeting. Keep a running record of what you delivered, with numbers, and what scope you took on beyond your original job description. Raise the topic before the budget cycle closes rather than after, since by review season the pool is usually already allocated.

Frame the request around expanded scope and demonstrated results, not around tenure or cost of living. "I have been here two years" is not an argument; "I now own the deployment pipeline and reduced release failures by half" is.

Changing employers remains the fastest route to a substantial compensation increase in most markets, and internal raises rarely match external offers for the same jump in seniority.
