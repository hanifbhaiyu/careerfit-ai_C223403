# Interview Preparation

## The stages and what each one tests

A technical hiring process usually has four stages, and candidates fail most often by preparing for the wrong one.

**Recruiter screen (20-30 minutes).** Tests whether the basic facts fit: notice period, salary expectation, location, visa status, and whether your stated experience matches the CV. It is not a technical test. Candidates lose here by being vague about their current salary situation or by giving a salary expectation before knowing the band.

**Technical screen (45-60 minutes).** A coding exercise or a discussion of fundamentals. For most local employers this means data structures, SQL, and language-specific questions rather than competitive-programming puzzles.

**Deep technical or system design (60-90 minutes).** Tests whether you can reason about a problem larger than a single function. Junior candidates are asked to explain their own projects in depth; mid-level and above are asked to design a system.

**Behavioural or cultural round.** Tests communication, ownership, and how you handle conflict and failure. Technically strong candidates are rejected here more often than they expect.

## Answering behavioural questions

Use a four-part structure. State the situation briefly, the task you owned, the actions you personally took, and the measurable result. Keep the situation to two sentences; most candidates spend three minutes on context and thirty seconds on their own contribution, which is the reverse of what the interviewer needs.

Say "I" rather than "we" when describing your actions. Interviewers cannot credit you for work you attribute to a team.

Prepare six stories before any interview, each with numbers attached:

1. A project you delivered end to end.
2. A technical decision you made and its tradeoffs.
3. A failure and what specifically changed in your practice afterwards.
4. A disagreement with a colleague and how it resolved.
5. A time you worked under a hard deadline.
6. Something you learned quickly under pressure.

These six stories, recombined, answer the large majority of behavioural questions. Rehearse them aloud, because a story that reads well silently often runs too long when spoken.

## Discussing your own projects

The most common failure among junior candidates is being unable to explain a project on their own CV. Interviewers probe exactly this, because it distinguishes candidates who built something from candidates who followed a tutorial.

For every project on your CV, be ready to answer:

- What problem does it solve, and for whom?
- Why did you choose this technology instead of the obvious alternative?
- What was the hardest bug, and how did you diagnose it?
- What would you rebuild differently now?
- How does it handle failure, scale, or bad input?

If you cannot answer these for a project, remove it from your CV. A project you cannot defend does more damage than an absent one.

## Technical fundamentals worth revising

For most roles, revision effort is best spent here rather than on advanced algorithms:

- Arrays, hash maps, strings, and the time complexity of operations on each.
- SQL joins, indexing, and why a query is slow.
- HTTP methods, status codes, and what actually happens when a request is made.
- The difference between authentication and authorisation.
- Git branching and how to resolve a merge conflict.
- In your primary language: memory model basics, error handling, and concurrency at a conceptual level.

Practise writing code by hand or in a plain editor without autocomplete, because that is the interview condition.

## Questions to ask the interviewer

Having no questions signals low interest. Ask things that a website cannot answer:

- What does the first ninety days look like for this role?
- What is the current biggest technical constraint on the team?
- How are decisions made when engineers disagree on approach?
- What has caused people to struggle in this role previously?

Avoid asking about leave policy or working hours in an early round. Those are offer-stage questions.

## After the interview

Send a short follow-up email within a day, referencing something specific from the conversation. It costs nothing and keeps you memorable in a shortlist discussion.

If rejected, ask once for specific feedback. Most companies decline, but the ones that answer give you the single most valuable input available for the next attempt.
