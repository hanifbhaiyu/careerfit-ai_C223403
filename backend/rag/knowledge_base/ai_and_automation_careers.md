# AI, Automation, and How Roles Are Changing

## What is actually shifting

Automation is changing the composition of technology work rather than removing it wholesale. The tasks most exposed are the repetitive and well-specified ones: boilerplate code, routine test writing, first-draft documentation, simple data entry and cleaning, and templated content production.

The tasks least exposed are those requiring judgement under ambiguity, accountability for outcomes, negotiation between stakeholders with conflicting goals, and physical or regulated work where a human must sign off.

For a candidate planning a career, the practical reading is not "avoid technology" but "avoid roles whose entire content is a well-specified repeatable task."

## What this means for entry-level work

The traditional entry route in software involved doing the simple, well-specified work while learning the system. That work is now partly automated, which raises the floor for a first hire. Employers increasingly expect junior candidates to arrive able to reason about a codebase rather than only to write functions from a specification.

The response that works: build things that are complete rather than exercises that are correct. A deployed application with users, error handling, and a documented decision about a tradeoff demonstrates the judgement that is now being screened for. Fifty completed tutorial exercises do not.

## Skills that gained value

**Reviewing and verifying generated output.** Code and analysis produced quickly still has to be judged correct. The ability to read unfamiliar code critically and spot a subtle error is now a more central skill than the ability to type it.

**Systems and integration thinking.** Understanding how components fit together, where failures propagate, and what happens under load is harder to automate than writing an individual function.

**Data literacy across roles.** Marketing, operations, finance, and HR roles increasingly expect the holder to query data directly rather than requesting a report.

**Communication and stakeholder management.** As individual output rises, the constraint shifts to deciding what should be built. That is a conversational and political skill.

**Domain depth.** Knowing a regulated or specialised field well enough to spot when a plausible-sounding output is wrong is durable and specific.

## Using AI tools in your own work and job search

Employers now generally expect familiarity with AI-assisted development tools, and in many teams it is assumed rather than asked about. Being able to describe how you use them, including where you do not trust them, reads as maturity in an interview.

For the job search itself, the useful applications are drafting and analysis rather than substitution:

- Drafting a first version of a cover letter that you then rewrite in your own voice.
- Practising interview answers aloud and getting feedback on structure.
- Summarising a job posting into the specific requirements to address.
- Identifying gaps between your CV and a posting.

The failure mode is submitting generated text unedited. Recruiters read hundreds of applications and recognise the register immediately, and an application that reads as unedited output signals low interest in the specific role. Generated material is a starting draft, never a submission.

## Positioning yourself

Two strategies work, and they pull in opposite directions.

**Specialise deeply** in an area where judgement and accountability matter: security, infrastructure, data engineering, regulated domains. These roles are hard to compress because being wrong is expensive.

**Broaden into the seam between technical and business work**: product, analytics, technical account management, solutions engineering. These roles are hard to compress because they involve deciding what to do rather than doing it.

The position that is genuinely exposed is the middle: a purely executional technical role, with no domain depth and no stakeholder responsibility, doing well-specified tasks. Candidates in that position should move in one of the two directions rather than waiting.

## A realistic view

Predictions about which jobs disappear have a poor track record, and the confident ones are usually selling something. What is reliably true is that the composition of tasks within a role changes faster than the role disappears, and that people who keep learning adapt while people who stop do not.

Plan for task change rather than for role extinction. The candidate who understands their domain, can verify what a tool produces, and can explain a decision to a non-technical stakeholder has a durable position regardless of which specific tools are current.
